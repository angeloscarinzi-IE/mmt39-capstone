# 09 · CEO Meeting 1 — Speaker notes

Company 3 · simulation `B1SIM2C2` · Year 1 review and Year 2 plan.
Organised **by presenter, in speaking order**. Slide numbers are binding and come from the
fixed 36-slide plan; every figure comes from the fact pack and must be read exactly as written.

**How to use this file.** Find your role, read your blocks in order, and read the `**Say:**`
text aloud more or less as written — it is drafted in full sentences so it can be delivered
under pressure. `**If asked:**` is the answer to give if the CEO interrupts on that slide.
`**Hand over:**` is the sentence that passes the floor.

> **The one rule that decides the grade.** The evaluation guidelines say in capitals that it
> is a **must to establish the CAUSES** for the results. Never state a number and stop. Say
> the number, then say **"because"**, then say the mechanism. "Share was 22.6%" scores badly.
> "Share was 22.6% **because** hypermarkets in A took 63,960 units against 2,057,439 in B"
> scores well. If you catch yourself about to say "and", say "because" instead.

## The whole story in 90 seconds

Any member must be able to deliver this if the CEO cuts the time. Learn it verbatim.

> We finished Year 1 **ranked first of five** on both net income and accumulated profit, with
> a net income of **€67,823,192** on net revenue of €163,704,462 — a 41.4% net margin against
> an industry average of **minus 20.2%**, in a year where two of our four rivals lost money.
> We got there because we priced sunscreen at €14.50, which was 71% above the cheapest rival,
> and still took 52.67% of market A and sold every unit we could make.
>
> We are not here to celebrate that, because the same year contains our one real error. We
> gave 22 of our 32 production lines to moisturiser and made 32,175,000 units against demand
> of 15,047,394, so **17,628,699 units are sitting in the warehouse** — €40,990,251 of cash
> converted into stock. At the same time sunscreen **sold out**, and the report shows
> **1,827,440 units of demand we could not serve**, worth €26,497,880 of revenue and
> €12,702,797 of contribution we simply did not collect. The plant was not the problem; it
> hit its forecast to the unit. The allocation of the plant was the problem.
>
> Year 2 fixes exactly that. We bought two SMED modules for €6,000,000, which lifts line
> utilisation from 81.25% to 92.9% and takes sunscreen from 5,850,000 to **6,688,800 units**.
> We activated 10 of 10 sunscreen lines and only **2 of 22** moisturiser lines, because we
> already own 17.6 million units of it — that single decision keeps €64.2 million of cash out
> of stock. We prepaid the whole €19,500,000 of remaining debt for a €195,000 penalty, so the
> financial line swings from minus €3,060,000 to **plus €55,000**.
>
> Our Year-2 forecast is a band, not a point: net income of **€82.5 million to €86.2 million**,
> centrally €84,074,528, which is **plus 24%** on Year 1. And we will tell you openly where we
> think we left money on the table, because we have costed it.

## Block 1 — slides 1 to 6 (4.5 minutes)

### Slide 1 — Title

**Say:** Good morning. We are Company 3 in simulation B1SIM2C2, and this is CEO Meeting 1,
covering our Year 1 results and our plan for Year 2. Before we start I will give you the
headline, because I would rather you heard it from us than found it on slide eight: we
finished Year 1 **ranked first of five** on net income and on accumulated profit, with a net
income of **€67,823,192**. We will spend most of the next forty minutes not on that number
but on why it happened, and on the one decision inside it that cost us more than any of our
competitors managed to cost us.

**If asked:** *"Are you here to tell me everything went well?"* — No. We made €67,823,192 of
net income against our own plan of €112,166,213. The whole of that €44 million gap has one
cause, and it is a decision we made ourselves: we built 32,175,000 units of moisturiser and
sold 14,546,301.

### Slide 2 — Agenda and speaking roles

**Say:** We will follow the six sections the guidelines set out. I will open with the
introduction — the market, our strategy and our goals. Our Finance Director then gives you
the summary of results. The longest section is section three, the analysis and the causes,
and all five of us present part of it, because the causes sit in four different departments.
We then give you our objectives, the actions we have already submitted for Year 2, and the
financial forecast that follows from them. We have kept our speaking time to thirty-eight
minutes so that you have at least seven minutes for questions, and we would rather you
interrupted than saved them.

**If asked:** *"Who is accountable for what?"* — Each slide carries the name of the director
who owns it in the top right corner, and the person who presents a decision is the person who
made it.

### Slide 3 — Divider: Introduction

**Say:** Section one: the introduction. Because this is our first meeting, we will spend two
minutes on the shape of the market itself, because every decision after this only makes sense
against it.

### Slide 4 — The market we compete in

**Say:** Five companies sold **51,380,110 units** across the two domestic markets in Year 1.
The important structural fact on this chart is that moisturiser is **74% of that volume** —
38,115,310 units against 13,264,800 for sunscreen — but it carries only €3.7849 of gross
profit per unit against €6.9511 for sunscreen. So the market gives you a choice between volume
and margin, and we chose margin. We sold **20,396,301 units in total, 39.70% of the whole
industry**, which is first place on combined volume. And the third market, the export market
E, is effectively unopened: the entire industry sold **98,165 units into it against 110,100,000
units of potential**, because only Company 1 and Company 2 entered at all and neither committed.

**If asked:** *"Why did you ignore market E entirely?"* — Because entering E costs platforms,
vehicles, a salesforce and dollar exposure, and Year 1 proved the price of staying out was
zero: the two companies that entered took 98,165 units between them, which is 0.09% of the
potential. We will revisit E when our domestic capacity is no longer the binding constraint.

### Slide 5 — The strategy: what kind of company we are

**Say:** Our strategy has three sentences and each one has evidence behind it. First, we are
**premium on sunscreen**: we priced at €14.50 flat in every channel, which was 71% above
Company 1 and Company 4 at €8.50, and we still took **52.67% of market A and sold out**. That
is not a claim, it is a measured result. Second, we treat **moisturiser as volume and cash**,
not as a margin product, because at €6.50 it earns €3.7849 a unit — and Year 1 showed us that
we ran that logic too far, which the analysis section will cover honestly. Third, we **scale A
and B before we touch E**, because we would rather own 39.70% of a proven 51.4 million-unit
market than buy a distribution network for a market the whole industry has left empty.

**If asked:** *"Is 'premium' just a word, or is it a position?"* — It is a measured position.
We are positioned at 1 on natural and 8 on technological for sunscreen, we run campaign 18,
and we charge the highest price in the industry. The market validated it by taking every unit
we made and leaving 1,827,440 units of demand unserved.

### Slide 6 — Goals: brand perception and market share

**Say:** Our brand goals are specific. On sunscreen we hold the technological position at
coordinates 1 and 8 and we hold the price premium, and our share goal for Year 2 is to stay
first in market A while converting the stockout into sales — we go from 5,850,000 units to
**6,688,800 units of supply**. On moisturiser we hold the natural position at 9 and 1, where
we are first in market B with **55.83% share**, and our goal in market A is to move from
**22.60%, which is third of three**, towards the high twenties. And I will flag now what
section three will prove: in market A we were the **cheapest moisturiser on the shelf and we
still finished third**, so our Year-2 goal there is deliberately modest, because we have bought
the research to find out why before we spend money guessing.

**If asked:** *"Why is your market-A moisturiser target so unambitious?"* — Because we do not
yet know the cause. We were the cheapest at €6.50 and finished third behind a company charging
€9.50 to €10.50. Setting a big target before we know the mechanism would be a guess, so we
have spent €680,600 on 22 surveys in Year 2 to identify it first.

**Hand over:** *"Our Finance Director will now take you through the summary of results."*
-> **Finance Director**

### Slide 9 — Sales and market share

**Say:** This is the whole picture in units. On **sunscreen** we sold 5,850,000 units, which is
**44.10% of the sunscreen market and first place**. Inside that, market A is 5,759,496 units
and **52.67% share, first**, while market B is only 90,504 units and 3.95%. On **moisturiser**
we sold 14,546,301 units, **38.16% and second overall**: market B is 9,989,807 units and
**55.83%, first**, while market A is 4,556,494 units and **22.60%, which is third**. Across
both products we sold 20,396,301 of the industry's 51,380,110 units — **39.70%, first**.

Two numbers on this slide explain the rest of the meeting, and both are causes, not outcomes.
**Sunscreen sold out**: the report shows demand for our brand of 7,677,440 units, so
**1,827,440 units went unserved** and our closing stock was zero. Our 3.95% in market B is
therefore not a price signal, it is a supply artefact — market A absorbed everything we made.
And on moisturiser the opposite happened: **17,628,699 units sat in stock** at year end and
501,093 units of demand still went unserved.

**If asked:** *"Why was market B only 3.95% on sunscreen? Did you under-advertise it?"* — No,
and that is an important negative finding. We ran identical media in both markets, twelve
insertions and €204,000 each, and B still took 90,504 units. Because we sold out nationally,
we cannot read B's price response at all this year — the demand was rationed away. We will get
a clean read in Year 2 when supply is 14% higher.

**If asked:** *"Is 501,093 units of unserved moisturiser not a distribution failure?"* — Every
logistic stockout flag in the report reads "No" for every market, so the fleet cleared. We
cannot explain those 501,093 units from the report and we are not going to invent a reason;
we have flagged it as an open question and bought the channel surveys to close it.

# 6 · MARKETING DIRECTOR

## Block 6 — slides 12 to 15 (4.5 minutes)

### Slide 12 — Portfolio, channels and positioning

**Say:** This is the single most important finding in our Year-1 analysis, and it is a channel
finding, not a price finding. In market A, hypermarkets took **63,960 units of our moisturiser
across 35 stores** — that is 1,827 units per store. In market B, hypermarkets took **2,057,439
units across 30 stores**, which is 68,581 per store. The same channel, the same product, the
same brand, and market B is **37 times better per store**. And it is not that the A hypermarkets
do not know us, **because** those same 35 stores took **2,587,071 units of our sunscreen** in
the same year. So the shelf is available to us and the buyer is willing.

On positioning, our moisturiser sits at 9 on natural and 1 on technological, and **Company 2
occupies the identical coordinates and runs the identical campaign, campaign 19**. That is our
working hypothesis for what happened: channel G behaves winner-take-most, and where two brands
present the same proposition, one of them takes the shelf.

**If asked:** *"If it is the channel, what are you doing about it?"* — In Year 2 we have bought
survey 15, shelf space achieved by company and channel, and survey 13, rival point-of-purchase
spend, precisely because we cannot currently see whether we lost the A hypermarket shelf or
never had it. Those two surveys cost a fraction of the roughly €12 million a year the gap is
worth.

**If asked:** *"Why did you not simply change positioning away from Company 2?"* — Because we
would be moving the one product that is **first in market B with 55.83% share** on that exact
positioning. Moving it to fix A would risk B, which is four times larger for us in units.

### Slide 13 — Price against competitors

**Say:** The price ladder shows two opposite results from the same pricing philosophy. On
**sunscreen** in market A we charged **€14.50 flat** against Company 1 and Company 4 at €8.50
and Company 2 at €11.50 to €12.50. That is **71% above the cheapest competitor**, and we
finished **first with 52.67% and sold out**. So on sunscreen, price premium is not a constraint;
supply is.

On **moisturiser** in market A we charged **€6.50 flat**, which was the **lowest or joint-lowest
price on the shelf**, and we finished **third with 22.60%**. Company 2 charged €9.50 to €10.50 —
three to four euros above us — and sold **7,782,836 units**. Company 4 charged €6.50 to €7.20
with a three-for-two promotion and sold **7,743,383 units**. We sold 4,556,494.

The conclusion we draw, and it drove our Year-2 pricing, is that **price is not the driver in
market A**. We were already the cheapest and it bought us third place, so cutting further would
have been spending margin on a lever that has already been shown not to work. That is why we
**raised** moisturiser in A from €6.50 to €7.50.

**If asked:** *"How can you raise the price of a product that is losing?"* — Because the €6.50
price point has already been tested and it produced 22.60% share. Moving to €7.50 recovers
€2,250,000 of contribution while we buy the research to find the real lever. If we cut instead,
we would be paying for a hypothesis the data already rejects.

**If asked:** *"Is €14.50 sunscreen too high or too low?"* — Too low, and we will quantify that
on slide 35. Demand for our brand was 7,677,440 units at €14.50 against capacity of 5,850,000.

### Slide 14 — Distribution, advertising, share of voice and shelf space

**Say:** Our total advertising including point-of-purchase was **€828,000 — media €678,000 and
POP €150,000 — which is 0.44% of sales at recommended price**. We ran campaign 18 on sunscreen
and campaign 19 on moisturiser, twelve insertions per market on sunscreen and eight on
moisturiser, and our net rating is already close to full coverage, so buying more weight would
have added cost without adding reach.

Here is the gap we have to own. We can tell you our own share of spend, but we **cannot tell you
our share of voice**, **because** we did not buy surveys 10, 11 and 13 in Year 1 — rival
advertising budgets, rival insertion counts and rival POP. We also did not buy survey 14, rival
salesforce, or survey 15, shelf space achieved. That is a real hole in a section the guidelines
ask about directly. We have fixed it: Year 2 research goes from €166,800 to **€680,600 and 22
surveys**, and it includes all five of those.

We ran **no promotions at all** in Year 1, while Company 2 ran a price reduction in every single
cell and Company 4 ran a three-for-two on moisturiser in supermarkets. That is the other lever we
cannot yet cost, and it is the one our Year-2 plan tests.

**If asked:** *"You are told to report share of voice. Why can you not?"* — Because we did not
buy the survey, and we would rather say that than estimate it. It is a €680,600 correction and
it is already made.

### Slide 15 — What our decisions did to demand

**Say:** This table is one row per lever, and it reads decision, effect, conclusion.

Sunscreen at €14.50 flat produced 52.67% share and a sell-out, so the conclusion is that the
premium is under-priced, not over-priced. Moisturiser at €6.50 in A produced third place at the
lowest price, so the conclusion is that price is not the lever there. Moisturiser at €7.50 in B
produced **55.83% and first place**, so the conclusion is that B is our strongest and most
price-tolerant position. Zero promotions produced no measurable loss on sunscreen, which sold
out anyway, but two rivals out-sold us on moisturiser in A while promoting, so the conclusion is
that promotion is worth one controlled test. And advertising held flat at €828,000 produced
first place on both products in their strong markets, so the conclusion is to hold it as a
control rather than change two variables at once.

**If asked:** *"Which of these are you actually changing in Year 2?"* — Price on both products,
and one promotion. We are deliberately holding advertising, positioning and shelf space constant
so that the price read comes back clean.

**Hand over:** *"Operations will explain what the plant did, and why the plan — not the plant —

## Block 7 — slides 16 to 18 (4.0 minutes)

### Slide 16 — Expected versus actual production

**Say:** I want to be direct about this slide, because it contains our worst decision of the
year. We ran **32 lines, which is the legal maximum — 10 on sunscreen and 22 on moisturiser —
at three shifts**. We produced **5,850,000 units of sunscreen and 32,175,000 units of
moisturiser**.

Now compare that with the demand the market actually had. Sunscreen demand was **7,677,440
units** and we made 5,850,000, so **1,827,440 units went unserved**. Moisturiser demand was
**15,047,394 units** and we made 32,175,000, so **17,628,699 units went into the warehouse**.
We gave **69% of the plant to the product that needed 32% of it**.

The critical point for you is what caused that, **because** it was not an execution failure.
Every line delivered exactly **195,000 sellable sunscreen units and 487,500 moisturiser units
per line-shift**, which is precisely 81.25% of nameplate, and our capacity forecast hit that to
the unit. The plant did exactly what we told it to do. **The plan was the error, not the
execution**, and that is a much easier thing to fix — it is a field on a form, and we have
already changed it.

**If asked:** *"What did that allocation cost you?"* — On sunscreen, 1,827,440 unserved units at
€6.9511 of gross profit is **€12,702,797 of contribution we did not collect**, and €26,497,880
of revenue. On moisturiser, €40,990,251 of cash is now sitting as finished goods. And §34 warns
that the brand damage from a stockout outlives the year in which we cause it.

**If asked:** *"Why not simply build more sunscreen lines?"* — We are at the 32-line legal cap.
That is why the Year-2 answer is SMED rather than capex on new lines: it buys output from lines
we already own.

### Slide 17 — Unit cost and productivity

**Say:** Our standard cost was **€5.5604 per unit of sunscreen and €2.3252 per unit of
moisturiser**, and all four components reproduce exactly. Raw material is €1.7335 and €1.1031,
which is the list price of €1.65 and €1.05 multiplied by 1.03 for our 120-day supplier terms and
by 1.02 for yield loss. Labour is €3.3231 and €0.9323, which is the crew cost divided across the
195,000 and 487,500 units a line-shift delivers. Power is €0.4411 and €0.2647, and repairs are
€0.0627 and €0.0251, at €12,224.10 per line-shift with preventive maintenance switched on.

Two productivity points. First, we ran at **81.25% of nameplate utilisation**, which is the
measured figure, not an estimate — that is the gap SMED closes in Year 2, taking us to 92.9%.
Second, **preventive maintenance pays twice**: it costs €500,000 a year, and it keeps the repairs
line at €0.0627 and €0.0251 a unit while removing breakdown risk on a plant that has no spare
capacity. With sunscreen sold out, an unplanned line stoppage is not a repair bill, it is lost
contribution at €6.9511 a unit.

**If asked:** *"Why three shifts rather than two?"* — Because labour is charged per line-shift and
sits inside standard cost, so cost per unit is identical at one, two or three shifts. The only
cost of three shifts is depreciation, and matching our output at two shifts would need 33 lines,
which breaches the 32-line cap.

**If asked:** *"Why no Poka Yoke?"* — It costs €720,000 per line and applies to every line ever
installed, so on 32 lines the payback runs beyond the length of the game.

### Slide 18 — Supply chain, raw material and transport

**Say:** On raw material, we purchased production multiplied by exactly **1.02**, we had **no
raw-material stockouts**, and every logistic stockout flag in the report reads "No" for every
market. Supply reliability was not a problem in Year 1.

Transport is a problem, and the guidelines ask us to report the deviation against the reference,
so here it is without softening. Total logistics was **€1,632,862**: factory to platform
€132,000, warehousing €600,862, and platform to retailer €900,000. Against the §10 and §12
references, our factory-to-platform leg ran at **€0.0065 per unit against a €0.006 reference,
which is 8% over and acceptable**. Our last-mile leg ran at **€0.0441 per unit against a €0.007
